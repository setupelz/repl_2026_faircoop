The question: fairness is assessed after the pathway is fixed

Global mitigation scenarios that inform climate ambition are built to find the least-cost route to a temperature goal, and fairness enters only afterwards, when analysts compare each region's modelled effort against a principle-based allocation and report the gap. Although this ex-post reading is well established, it produces a familiar conclusion in which fair effort-sharing and urgent collective action appear to be in tension, and the finance required to close the gap reads as a tax on ambition.

We ask what changes when fairness is a design constraint rather than an ex-post check. That is, we test whether a pathway to 1.5 °C or 2 °C survives once principle-based 'fair shares' are imposed at the point where the scenario is generated and regions are allowed to cooperate to meet them, and how such a pathway differs from the cost-effective one it started from.

Approach: a framework that starts from any existing scenario

The framework takes a globally cost-effective source scenario and splits its emissions into a covered set, allocated to the twelve model regions by a fairness principle, and an excluded set (land use, international shipping and aviation) that keeps the source carbon price. Regions that have already used more than their allocation carry a 'carbon debt', which they must close through some combination of deeper domestic gross reductions, domestic carbon removal, and cooperation with other regions, with cooperation solved inside MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM-GAINS so that the price of transferred allocation emerges from the model rather than being imposed on it.

Two corner solutions bound the space, since under unlimited transfers regions may buy their way to compliance and the physical pathway reproduces the source scenario, whereas under lowest-feasible transfers finance is held to the minimum the model needs to remain feasible, starting from the roughly 100 billion USD per year already committed. Building on this, we explore six allocation approaches (equal and capability-adjusted cumulative per capita, from 1990, 2015, or 2025), a removal-only cooperation mechanism, a ten-year delay, and two carbon budgets.

Both budgets run from 2020 and allow overshoot, with 800 Gt CO2 consistent with limiting warming to approximately 2 °C at 67% likelihood and 500 Gt CO2 consistent with 1.5 °C at 50% likelihood, in each case by exceeding the limit and returning to it.

Findings: the climate outcome holds while the fossil exit accelerates

We find that both transfer corners reproduce the source scenario's end-of-century net cumulative CO2, indicating that fairness and ambition are not inherently in tension, and that global renewables deployment and electrification are largely indistinguishable across variants, so that what moves is the fossil exit. When transfers are held to lowest-feasible volumes, higher-responsibility regions reduce faster at home and global fossil primary energy in 2040 falls 3 to 21% below the unlimited-transfers case depending on the allocation approach, while the transfer obligation of 10.1 to 44.8 trillion USD (NPV, 2026 to 2100) implied by unlimited transfers is cut by more than half.

The aggregate cost is modest and its distribution progressive, in that against a cost-effective 2 °C scenario that lowers global consumption by approximately 0.8% relative to no new policy, the fair-share variants raise this to approximately 1.0%, and to at most 1.5% under lowest-feasible transfers, while lower-responsibility regions largely gain relative to that cost-effective pathway. Notably, restricting cooperation to financing geological carbon removal costs approximately 220 USD per tonne of mitigation transferred, against 20 to 30 USD when all mitigation options are in scope, and delaying cooperation by a decade roughly triples the near-term increase in global energy investment. Under a 1.5 °C budget the solution space persists, with differentiation settled increasingly through finance.

Why it matters: fairness as a feature of feasibility, not a constraint on ambition

None of these dynamics are visible in ex-post fair-share assessments of cost-effective scenarios, and they emerge only when the scenario generation process itself accounts for differentiated responsibilities and capabilities. Under constrained finance, a 'gross emissions reductions first' strategy among higher-responsibility regions appears to release pressure on lower-responsibility regions, and the window for regional differentiation remains open even where the remaining 1.5 °C budget is exhausted before the first model step.

The results quantify the aggregate potential of interregional cooperation, market and non-market, and neither prescribe carbon market designs nor endorse Article 6 mechanisms to cover NDC shortfalls. They also externalise climate damages and rest on two principle families in one model. In summary, we argue that scenario assessments should routinely explore fair-share variants, and lowest-feasible transfers in particular, alongside cost-effective pathways as the next NDC cycle and the second Global Stocktake approach.

What the explorer shows

The Explorer tab draws the paper's scenario set on indicators of climate action: total emissions, renewable capacity, energy efficiency and electrification, fossil fuel supply, energy-system and non-CO2 emissions, coal power, and emissions by demand sector. On every card the black line is the cost-optimal source pathway, orange the unlimited transfers corner and blue the lowest transfers corner, for the region chosen in the control bar. The strip above the cards shows the cumulative CO2 every line reaches. The drawer adds the other allocation approaches, the removal-only mechanism, the delay and the discount-rate case as further pairs of corners, and the budget switch moves the whole page to the 1.5 °C set. The reference run is the CMIP7 Low marker pathway from the same model, for comparison with a scenario built without fair shares.
